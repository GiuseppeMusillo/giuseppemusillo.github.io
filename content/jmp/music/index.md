**Abstract**

What happens when an expertise-leveling technology diffuses through an industry? I study digital sampling, which makes recorded performances reusable and reduces the expertise required for music production. I assemble novel data on production credits, sampling, and content for 70,712 US records released between 1980 and 2000. A difference-in-differences design identifies the causal effects of geographic exposure to a new sampler’s 1988 promotional campaign attached to Michael Jackson’s tour. Campaign exposure increased sampling. Main artists on records using sampling were more likely to be self-taught and less likely to report formal musical training. Participation in music production, measured as participants per 100,000 working-age residents, showed no detectable change. On records produced in exposed commuting zones, the number of musicians working exclusively in creative authorship and production grew by 0.5, roughly 15 percent of the pre-period mean, with no detectable broadening of individual task bundles. Records became more acoustically differentiated from recent music before the organizational response emerged, while lyrics showed no significant change. Lowering the expertise required for production can change the role of specialists and the characteristics of creative output, rather than simply enabling individuals to do more themselves.
