abstract: |
  How does violent state repression affect political participation in a mature democracy? We study the 2001 G8 summit in Genova, Italy, where police attacked authorized protests and committed abuses that courts later ruled met the international definition of torture. We measure each municipality's exposure by its proximity to the associations that organized travel to Genova. Exposure lowers turnout in national elections by 0.62 percentage points per standard deviation. The gradient is still visible in 2022, two decades after the summit. The effect is withdrawal, not realignment: it raises abstention among former voters, first on the left, and does not show up in petitions, demonstrations, or local and European elections. Two patterns point to a repression narrative that spread by word of mouth against a competing official account. The decline reaches one percentage point in rural municipalities and persists through 2013, while the urban effect fades to zero, and it shrinks where the reception of the mainstream television signal was stronger. Trust falls for the central institutions of the national state, and rises for interpersonal trust and local belonging. Repression cut the tie between citizens and the state, not the ties among citizens.
